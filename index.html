The thesis

Generation got cheap. Verification did not.

For thirty years, writing software was the bottleneck and testing was the tax you paid at the end. That equation has inverted. A model can now produce ten thousand plausible lines before lunch — syntactically clean, well-typed, confidently commented, and occasionally solving a problem nobody asked it to solve.

Automated checks confirm that the code does what the code says. They cannot confirm that the code does what the business meant. Coverage metrics inherit the blind spots of the generator, because increasingly the tests were generated too. A suite that grades its own homework is not a safety net; it is a mirror.

The scarce resource in AI-era software is not people who can write code. It is people who can tell you, credibly, that it is safe to ship.

That work is stubbornly human. It means reading the intent behind a requirement, noticing the edge case that never made it into a ticket, recognizing that a flow is technically correct and practically hostile, and being willing to say no with a name attached. Exploratory testing, accessibility judgment, domain intuition, adversarial curiosity — none of it reduces neatly to a prompt.

Which points at a market: distributed networks of skilled human testers, credentialed and rated, brought in on demand to audit machine-written work. Part marketplace, part assurance layer, part professional guild. Whatever it ends up being called, it needs a name people already understand.
